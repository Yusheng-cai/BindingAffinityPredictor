Protein–ligand modeling · field guide

Affinity is not one prediction.

A pose, a binder probability, an IC50, a Kd, and a binding free energy answer different scientific questions. This guide compares current models by their real inputs, outputs, and evidence—not by a single headline score.

Start with the endpoint.
Before asking which model is “best,” ask what quantity was measured, how the test was split, and whether the benchmark resembles the chemistry you care about. LITERATURE SNAPSHOT · 2026-08-09

The central rule: compare models only on a shared experimental endpoint and a shared test set. Pearson r on a congeneric ligand series, AUROC on binders versus decoys, and ligand RMSD are not interchangeable measures of “affinity accuracy.”

01 · Read the output label

Five outputs that are often blurred together

These quantities are related, but they require different labels, assumptions, and benchmarks. Moving from left to right generally asks a more quantitative thermodynamic question.

GEOMETRY Binding pose

Where the ligand sits and how the atoms are arranged. A correct pose can still have a wrong affinity.

coordinates, RMSD
CLASSIFICATION Binder probability

A ranking score for active versus inactive compounds. It is not a concentration or equilibrium constant.

P(binder)
POTENCY IC50 / EC50

Assay-dependent concentrations giving 50% inhibition or response. Conditions and mechanism matter.

log10(IC50/µM)
EQUILIBRIUM Kd / Ki

Kd describes binding equilibrium; Ki describes inhibition under a mechanistic model.

pKd = −log10(Kd/M)
THERMODYNAMICS ΔG° / ΔΔG

Absolute standard binding free energy or a free-energy change between two related ligands.

kcal mol⁻¹

y = log10(IC50 / µM). Lower values indicate greater predicted potency. Try −3, 0, or 2.

02 · Where structure models fit

From protein shape to binding thermodynamics

“Structure-based” covers several different tasks. A method may describe a protein surface, find a pocket, generate a ligand pose, score a static complex, or sample a thermodynamic ensemble. Those are not equivalent predictions.

1 · TARGET STRUCTURE

What shape can the protein adopt?

Experimental structures or predictors provide the 3D receptor. An apo structure is not guaranteed to contain the ligand-bound pocket conformation.

PDBAlphaFoldESMFold
2 · SURFACE / SITE

Where could an interaction occur?

Protein-only models annotate geometric and chemical patterns on the surface or identify likely ligand-binding residues. They usually do not place a specified ligand.

MaSIF / dMaSIFAF2BIND
3 · POSE / COMPLEX

How might this ligand bind?

Docking or cofolding generates relative protein–ligand coordinates. Flexible methods may also alter side chains or the protein backbone.

DiffDockDynamicBindBoltz-2
4 · LEARNED SCORE

Does the pose resemble labeled binders?

A supervised head maps learned features to binder labels, potency, or affinity-like values. The training assay defines the meaning of the number.

Boltz-2Nesso-1FlashAffinityPIGNet2
5 · ENSEMBLE PHYSICS

What is the free-energy difference?

MD and alchemical workflows sample solvent, protein, and ligand configurations under a force field to estimate ΔG or ΔΔG.

OpenFEFEP+

MaSIF stops mainly at stage 2. It can supply surface fingerprints or interaction-site hypotheses to later design, docking, or scoring workflows, but it does not natively predict a ligand pose, Kd, IC50, or binding free energy. Boltz-2 instead jumps from molecular inputs to stages 3 and 4 in one model.

03 · Model explorer

Representative models and workflows

Boltz-2, Nesso-1, and FlashAffinity are the affinity starting set. MaSIF and DynamicBind show two distinct meanings of structure-based learning: surface understanding and ligand-specific complex generation.

MaSIF family

2020–2021 · peer reviewed
protein surfacespeer reviewed

Molecular Surface Interaction Fingerprinting learns local geometric and chemical patterns on a protein’s solvent-excluded surface, rather than treating the protein only as a residue sequence or atom graph.

Input
A protein 3D structure. Original MaSIF builds a triangulated surface mesh and local geodesic patches; dMaSIF creates a differentiable surface point cloud directly from atomic coordinates and chemical types.
Native outputs
MaSIF-ligand: logits for seven cofactor classes in an already defined pocket. MaSIF-site: protein–protein interaction propensity per surface vertex. MaSIF-search: patch fingerprints for surface matching.
Best-fit use
Protein function and interface analysis, pocket characterization, PPI search, and protein-binder design.
Does not provide
An arbitrary small-molecule pose, a compound-specific affinity ranking, Kd, IC50, or ΔG.
Comparison rule: evaluate MaSIF on its native classification, PPI-site, or surface-retrieval task. The released MaSIF-ligand classifier covers ADP, CoA, FAD, haem, NAD, NADP, and SAM—not arbitrary drug-like ligands or galactose. The original code also uses an older TensorFlow and surface-preprocessing stack; dMaSIF removes much of that precomputation but was demonstrated on PPI tasks.

DynamicBind

2024 · peer reviewed
flexible dockingpeer reviewed

A ligand-specific structural model that starts from an apo-like receptor and jointly adjusts ligand translation, rotation, torsions, and protein conformation toward a predicted holo complex.

Input
An apo-like protein structure, often an AlphaFold prediction, plus ligand SMILES or SDF.
Native output
Sampled protein–ligand complex coordinates, a confidence score, and a PDBbind-trained affinity score.
Best-fit use
Pose generation when induced fit, alternate receptor states, or cryptic pockets may matter.
Not the same as
An equilibrium ensemble or rigorous free-energy calculation; generated states are learned structural hypotheses.
Authors report: pose and pocket recovery on chronological PDBbind and major-drug-target sets, plus mean AUROC = 0.68 on a 12-target antibiotics screen. These pose, screening, and affinity tasks must remain separate in our evaluation.
papercode

Boltz-2

2025 · open-source preprint
full-atom cofoldingpreprint

Jointly predicts a protein–ligand complex, structure confidence, binder likelihood, and an IC50-like continuous score from biomolecular inputs.

Input
Protein sequence, ligand SMILES/CCD, usually an MSA; optional templates or pocket constraints.
Native output
Full-atom coordinates, confidence arrays, P(binder), and log10(IC50/µM).
Best-fit use
Pose hypothesis plus binder triage and ranking related active molecules.
Not the same as
An equilibrium Kd measurement or explicit-solvent free-energy calculation.
Authors report: average Pearson r = 0.62 on the 876-pair OpenFE subset and 0.66 on the 87-ligand, four-kinase subset; also CASP16 affinity and MF-PCBA classification. Target sequence holdout does not guarantee novel ligand chemistry.

Nesso-1

2026 · open-source preprint
coarse cofoldingpreprint

Keeps token-level protein–ligand geometry but removes Boltz-2’s heavy-atom diffusion decoder. It uses ESM-2 embeddings and requires no MSA.

Input
Protein sequence and ligand SMILES, CCD, or SDF; ESM-2 embeddings are generated or supplied.
Native output
Coarse pair-distance distributions and entropy, P(binder), and log10(IC50/µM).
Best-fit use
High-throughput affinity ranking when an all-atom complex is not required.
Structural limit
No atomistic protein pose or resolved binding-site side-chain geometry.
Authors report: weighted Pearson r = 0.80 (FEP+ four), 0.66 (OpenFE), and 0.68 (CASP16), plus best reported AI results on the 494-compound OpenBind set. Their report explicitly labels the first three benchmarks largely in-distribution and reports >10× speedup over Boltz-2 on an H100, excluding fixed per-target costs.

FlashAffinity / FlashBind

2025 · preprint / weights
dock then scorepreprint

The paper calls the scorer FlashAffinity; related weights and assets are released under FlashAffinity/FlashBind. It trades full cofolding for a docking stage followed by a PairFormer/EGNN-style scorer.

Input
Protein sequence plus ligand SMILES, or a supplied complex. The full pipeline retrieves/predicts a receptor structure and docks the ligand before scoring.
Native output
Continuous affinity regression and binary binder classification; it is a scorer, not an alchemical ΔG engine.
Best-fit use
Fast virtual-screening prioritization when a usable receptor structure and pocket are known.
Main dependency
Performance can be limited by docking pose and receptor-state quality.
Authors report: Pearson r = 0.53 on the four-target FEP+ test, CASP16 Kendall τ = 0.51, and MF-PCBA target-average AUROC = 0.776. Their L40S timing is 0.7 s per full-pipeline pair versus 35 s for their Boltz-2 baseline; timings are hardware and protocol specific.

PIGNet2

2024 · peer reviewed
physics-informed scorepeer reviewed

A 3D graph neural scoring function with physically motivated interaction components, trained for affinity scoring and active/decoy or pose discrimination.

Input
A prepared 3D protein–ligand complex or docked poses.
Native output
A learned binding-affinity score and virtual-screening ranking.
Best-fit use
Rescoring known or docked complex geometries and inspecting interaction contributions.
Main dependency
Requires a pose; static complexes omit explicit ensemble, solvent, and entropy sampling.
Evidence: evaluated on all four CASF-2016 “powers” and on larger DUD-E/DEKOIS active–decoy screens. CASF scoring uses crystal poses and should not be equated with blind pose generation or novel-target generalization.
papercode

DrΔG-Net

2024 · peer reviewed
3D directional GNNpeer reviewed

A useful label-audit case: despite the “ΔG” name, the published readouts regress to pKi. The model can be transferred to a local ligand series using a small calibration set.

Input
3D protein-pocket and ligand atomic coordinates; a ligand-only variant was also tested.
Native output
pKi, linearly related to ΔG° at fixed temperature but still an experimentally trained label.
Best-fit use
Global scoring followed by target- or series-specific transfer learning.
Scientific lesson
Names and energy-like architecture do not determine the supervised endpoint.
Reported test: CASF-2016 RMSE = 1.36 pK units. A ligand-only version reached 1.43, warning that familiar benchmarks may reward ligand-set correlations or leakage more than protein-specific physics.
papercode

PBCNet2.0

2026 · peer reviewed
relative affinitypeer reviewed

A Siamese 3D network designed to compare two ligands bound to the same target—a question closer to medicinal-chemistry optimization than global absolute scoring.

Input
A pair of protein–ligand complex structures for related chemical alternatives.
Native output
Relative binding-affinity change / ordering between the pair.
Best-fit use
Prioritizing local chemical modifications within a target program.
Not designed for
Universal absolute affinity from an arbitrary sequence–ligand pair.
Evidence: the 2026 study reports retrospective and prospective tests, including ENPP1 and ALDH1B1. This paired setting is conceptually comparable to RBFE ranking, but the network is learned rather than a thermodynamic simulation.
paper

OpenFE

current · non-AI reference
alchemical simulation

An open physical workflow, included as the thermodynamic reference point. It samples force-field models across alchemical states instead of learning a direct mapping from structures to assay labels.

Input
Carefully prepared receptor, bound poses, ligand states, atom mappings, solvent, force field, and protocol.
Native output
RBFE ΔΔG between ligands; current workflows also support ABFE ΔG with statistical uncertainties.
Best-fit use
Lead optimization for a limited, related ligand series after careful system preparation.
Tradeoff
Much more compute and expertise; errors arise from sampling, force fields, protonation, and setup.
Comparison rule: RBFE and potency models can both rank a congeneric series, but their native quantities and uncertainty models differ. Benchmark them against the same assay measurements—not just against each other.

04 · Benchmark literacy

The same model can look different on each test

A benchmark is a scientific question encoded as a dataset, split, and metric. Its limitations travel with every number reported on it.

Surface / site prediction

MaSIF-ligand classifies known pockets; MaSIF-site labels protein–protein interface surface vertices. AF2BIND instead predicts small-molecule-contact residues.

METRIC · AUROC/AUPRC, top-k recall, pocket class

Supports: whether a model localizes or characterizes a site. Limit: it does not test ligand pose or affinity, and nearby homologous surfaces can leak across splits.

FEP+ / OpenFE series

OpenFE subset: 876 protein–ligand pairs. A smaller set has 87 neutral ligands across CDK2, TYK2, JNK1, and p38.

METRIC · within-assay Pearson r, RMSE

Supports: ranking related ligands within targets. Limit: recent analyses find high chemical similarity to common public training data.

CASP16 affinity

A blind-at-the-time affinity challenge with 140 protein–ligand complexes—but only two target systems.

METRIC · correlation / rank by target

Supports: a controlled blind case. Limit: two targets cannot establish broad target-family generalization.

MF-PCBA

A large active/inactive benchmark intended to resemble high-throughput hit identification, with up to millions of interactions.

METRIC · AP, AUROC, EF at top x%

Supports: binder enrichment. Limit: says nothing direct about continuous potency error among actives.

CASF-2016

285 high-quality PDBbind core complexes grouped into 57 clusters, with scoring, ranking, docking, and screening tasks.

METRIC · Pearson, Spearman, pose and screen success

Supports: standardized scoring-function tests. Limit: crystal-pose scoring and PDBbind-derived training can invite similarity leakage.

OpenBind affinity

494 compounds against EV-A71 2A protease, a flexible target with an apo-to-holo loop change and less familiar chemistry.

METRIC · RMSE, Spearman ρ

Supports: a harder zero-shot campaign-like test. Limit: one target; molecular weight was a surprisingly strong baseline.

Prospective assay

Predictions are frozen before new compounds are synthesized and measured using a specified biochemical or biophysical assay.

METRIC · decision utility + calibrated error

Supports: real operational value. Limit: expensive, small, and often proprietary—but scientifically the most persuasive.

Why there is no honest single leaderboard

Boltz-2’s OpenFE Pearson, FlashAffinity’s MF-PCBA AUROC, PIGNet2’s CASF docking success, and an OpenFE ΔΔG RMSE describe different endpoints and evaluation units. Even two Pearson values can differ in target weighting, assay grouping, test chemistry, and training overlap. Treat paper scores as claims tied to a protocol, not permanent properties of a model.

05 · Concrete example

Our galectin-3–galactose smoke test

This one-case run checks that the Boltz-2 pipeline works and asks whether it can recover a known binding geometry. It is not yet an affinity benchmark.

Human galectin-3 CRD + β-D-galactose

Boltz-2 received the protein sequence and ligand identity without the reference complex coordinates. We compared the predicted structure with PDB 9D63 afterward.

Reference: 1.15 Å X-ray structure. The experiment intentionally has no validated affinity label for free galactose.

Ligand pose RMSD0.475 Å
Binder probability0.575
Predicted y2.104

What we can say: this sample recovered the crystallographic ligand pose closely after protein alignment, and the pipeline emitted plausible files and scores.

What we cannot say: that ~127 µM is accurate. There is no matched experimental IC50 label here, and pose RMSD does not validate potency or ΔG°.

Would MaSIF help this particular test?

Not as a galactose-affinity model. Galectin-3’s structure could be converted to a molecular surface for exploratory fingerprints, but the released MaSIF-ligand task neither discovers an arbitrary small-molecule pocket nor includes galactose among its seven ligand classes. A cleaner structural-site test would give an apo or predicted galectin-3 structure to a general pocket predictor such as AF2BIND, then compare its predicted residues with the ligand-contact residues in PDB 9D63. Boltz-2 would remain the pose and affinity-like model.

06 · Our benchmark ladder

A small test that can grow into evidence

The first useful comparison should be narrow and reproducible: one endpoint, one assay family, a frozen split, and baselines simple enough to expose bias.

Define the claim

Pose recovery, binder enrichment, absolute potency, or within-series ranking—choose one.

Freeze labels

Use one assay type where possible; retain units, conditions, censoring, and provenance.

Control leakage

Audit both protein similarity and ligand/scaffold similarity to every model’s training data.

Add baselines

Include molecular weight, ligand-only fingerprints, docking, and a physics method when appropriate.

Measure uncertainty

Report confidence intervals, per-target results, failures, and sensitivity to seeds or poses.

Test prospectively

Eventually freeze predictions before measurement; retrospective success is hypothesis generation.
